The Mandate

The company had identified AI as a strategic priority but lacked the operational layer to turn that into shipped, adopted products. Two teams — customer support and content — were burning time on low-leverage work: agents searching across fragmented systems to answer questions they already knew, and writers spending their best hours producing first drafts instead of directing creative strategy.

My role as AI Portfolio Manager was to define what got built, sequence the work, align stakeholders, and — critically — own adoption until each system became the default way teams operated. Building the tools was never the hard part. Getting 50 people to change how they work every day was.

"The goal wasn't to automate tasks. It was to give decision-making back to the people closest to the work — and remove the friction that was slowing them down."

What I Owned End-to-End

Most AI projects fail at the handoff between "built" and "used." My role was designed to close that gap — I held accountability from the first stakeholder conversation through to daily active usage.

System 01 — Customer Service Knowledge Assistant

The Ops Problem Behind the Tech Problem

Agents weren't slow because they lacked knowledge — they were slow because that knowledge lived in five different places and required a supervisor to interpret policy edge cases. Every escalation was a tax on the team's throughput and a signal that decision authority hadn't been properly distributed.

I scoped this as an operations problem first: how do we make correct, policy-aligned decisions accessible to the person handling the customer, in real time, without requiring a supervisor? The RAG system was the answer — but the real work was defining what "unified knowledge" meant, aligning the support team on the new workflow, and iterating until escalations actually dropped.

1

Problem Definition & Stakeholder Buy-In

Mapped the escalation patterns to identify root causes. Built the business case around decision latency — not automation — which got support leadership aligned on the investment before engineering was involved.

2

Knowledge Architecture & Source Unification

Coordinated ingestion of PDFs, internal wikis, SOPs, and live CRM data into a single vector store. The ops challenge was deciding what belonged in the system, what didn't, and how to handle conflicting policy versions.

3

Workflow Integration & Agent Onboarding

Designed the adoption rollout — not just training sessions, but rebuilding the daily workflow around the new tool so agents experienced immediate time savings from day one. Feedback loops ran weekly through launch.

4

Outcome Tracking & Continuous Calibration

Monitored escalation rates, query quality, and agent confidence post-launch. When edge cases surfaced, I coordinated prompt and knowledge base updates — keeping the system calibrated to real operational needs.

System 02 — AI Copilot for Content Operations

Shifting a Team's Operating Model, Not Just Their Tools

The content team's bottleneck wasn't creativity — it was time allocation. Writers were spending the majority of their hours producing first drafts: the lowest-leverage part of the content production cycle. Strategy, iteration, and brief quality suffered as a result.

I framed this to leadership as an operating model problem: if we could shift writers from execution to supervision, we'd unlock capacity for the work that actually required human judgment. The AI system was the mechanism — the ops design was the intervention.

1

Operating Model Design

Mapped the existing content workflow, identified where AI could absorb volume work, and redesigned the writer's role around brief quality and creative direction — before any tool was built or selected.

2

System Scoping & Model Evaluation

Evaluated Claude, GPT, and Gemini across tone, brand alignment, and output consistency for the content team's specific formats. Defined the prompting architecture around brand voice, past performance data, and campaign context.

3

Role Transition & Change Management

The hardest part wasn't the tool — it was helping writers redefine what good work looked like in the new model. I ran structured sessions to shift the team from "I write" to "I direct," with clear quality standards for AI output review.

↑ Output
Content throughput increased significantly — more posts shipped per week without growing the team
↑ Strategy
Writers redirected freed capacity toward campaign strategy, brief quality, and creative direction

Why Both Systems Reached Daily Adoption

Most AI pilots die between launch and habit. The gap isn't technical — it's operational. These systems reached daily active usage because adoption was treated as a first-class deliverable, not an afterthought.

🔑
AI value is unlocked at the ops layer, not the model layer. The choice of Claude vs GPT vs Gemini mattered far less than how clearly we defined the workflow, the role change, and the success criteria for the teams using these systems daily.
🎯
Adoption is a strategy problem, not a training problem. We didn't run training sessions and hope behavior changed. We rebuilt the daily workflow so the new system was the path of least resistance from day one.
📐
Human-in-the-loop design preserves trust. AI handled volume and retrieval. Humans retained judgment and escalation authority. That distinction was explicit — and it was what made teams willing to rely on the system.
🔁
Post-launch calibration is where the real ops work happens. Both systems improved most in the 4 weeks after launch. Weekly feedback loops, prompt updates, and workflow refinements are what separated "used once" from "used every day."
