The Timing Pattern.
How a Data Insight Changed a Fintech's Collections Strategy.

At RappiCard México, I ran a Champion-Challenger analysis that uncovered a timing pattern in borrower behavior — showing that treatment path effectiveness depended entirely on when, not just how, we reached customers after first contact. The finding changed how the team made collections decisions and moved repayment funnel conversion.

The Problem the Data Revealed

The collections team had a working process — after first contact with a delinquent borrower, agents followed a standard treatment path: offer a payment plan, escalate to hard collection, or move toward write-off. The assumption was that treatment type drove outcomes.

What the data actually showed was different. Treatment type mattered less than most assumed. What mattered — and what no one had measured — was timing. The same treatment applied at different points in the delinquency curve produced dramatically different repayment rates. We had been treating a timing problem as a treatment problem.

"The insight wasn't that we needed different strategies — it was that the right strategy at the wrong moment was nearly as costly as the wrong strategy entirely."

The Experiment — Champion vs Challenger

How the Analysis Was Structured

I designed the Champion-Challenger framework to test what was actually driving repayment — isolating treatment path from timing so we could measure each independently. The Champion was the existing approach; the Challenger introduced timing-based routing as the primary decision variable.

Champion — Existing
Treatment-first routing
After first contact, agents selected treatment path (payment plan, hard collection, or write-off) based on account balance and prior contact history. Timing was not a structured variable.
Baseline conversion rate

Challenger — New
Timing-first routing
Treatment path selection gated by delinquency stage. Payment plan offers prioritized in early windows; hard collection reserved for specific later stages. Timing became the first decision, treatment second.

The Pattern — Timing Windows

What the Data Actually Showed

When I segmented the repayment data by delinquency stage rather than treatment type, a clear pattern emerged. Borrower receptiveness to payment plan offers dropped sharply after a specific window — and hard collection actions applied too early were counterproductive, reducing repayment probability rather than increasing it.

Think of it like fruit ripeness: the same action — offering a payment plan — has completely different outcomes depending on where the borrower is in their delinquency curve. Too early and they're not alarmed enough to act. Too late and the relationship has broken down. There was a window, and we hadn't been targeting it.

The Impact — How Decisions Changed

From Analysis to Strategy Shift

The finding had to travel from a SQL query to a changed operational process — which meant translating it for the collections team, the credit risk lead, and ultimately leadership. I built the SQL models and dashboards that made the pattern visible, then framed the recommendation around what the team needed to do differently on Monday morning, not just what the data said.

What This Taught Me About Data & Decisions

🔍
The real insight is usually one layer deeper than the first cut. The first analysis confirmed treatment paths mattered. The second — segmented by timing — is where the real pattern lived. Most analysts stop at the first answer.
⏱️
Timing is a strategic variable, not a scheduling detail. In collections, in product, in almost any operations context — when you do something matters as much as what you do. The data made that concrete.
📊
A dashboard that doesn't change a decision is just a report. The real deliverable wasn't the SQL model or the Power BI view — it was the routing policy change. The analytics were the mechanism, not the outcome.
🎯
Frame findings in terms of Monday morning, not the model. Leadership didn't need to understand the Champion-Challenger methodology. They needed to know: here's what we change, here's why, here's what we expect to happen.
