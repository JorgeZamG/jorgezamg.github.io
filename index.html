Operations 3 min read

Why most dashboards get ignored — and how to fix that.

After building dashboards at HP, RappiCard, and EY, I noticed a pattern: the ones that get ignored all share the same design failure. It's not about aesthetics — it's about who you built it for.

The mistake I see most often is building a dashboard for the person who requested it, not the person who will use it daily. A VP asks for a portfolio health view. You build something beautiful with 12 KPIs. The VP opens it once, nods, and moves on. The analyst who needs it every morning never adopts it because the metrics don't match how they actually think about their work.

At RappiCard, I rebuilt our collections dashboard by spending two days shadowing the ops team before writing a single query. I asked: what decision do you make at 9am? What would make that decision take 30 seconds instead of 20 minutes? The result was a dashboard with 4 metrics and one drill-down. It was used every single day.

The rule I follow now: if a dashboard needs a tutorial, it's already failed. Build for the 9am decision, not the quarterly review.

AI & Automation 3 min read

I automated 2 hours of daily crypto research. Here's what I built.

Every morning I used to manually check price feeds, scan whale wallets, and read protocol updates across Solana and Ethereum. I don't do that anymore — an n8n pipeline does it for me at 6:30am.

The pipeline pulls data from three sources: CoinGecko for price and volume signals, Solscan for whale wallet movement on Solana, and a curated set of Ethereum RPC calls for L2 activity. Every data point gets routed into a single Claude API prompt that I spent two weeks iterating on.

The prompt doesn't just summarize — it flags anomalies, compares against 7-day baselines, and surfaces anything that crosses a threshold I defined. The output lands in Telegram and email at 6:30am and 6:30pm UTC. Five people use it daily.

What I learned: the hardest part wasn't the automation — it was defining what "signal" vs. "noise" actually means. That's a human judgment call, and no API can make it for you. The pipeline only works because I spent time first answering: what would actually change my decision today?

AI in Enterprise 3 min read

Running AI tools inside enterprise teams — what nobody tells you.

I managed AI projects across global teams. The technical part was the easy bit. The hard part was everything that happens after you deploy.

Nobody warns you that the real adoption blockers aren't technical. They're social. The analyst who's been doing things manually for 4 years isn't resistant to AI — they're resistant to being wrong in front of their team. The tool needs to make them look good, not replace them.

The pattern I found that works: find one early adopter on each team who's already curious, give them early access, and let them become the internal champion. By the time you roll out to the full team, half of them have already heard "this thing actually works" from a peer they trust. That's worth more than any training session.

The real lesson: deploying AI in an enterprise is a change management problem dressed up as a technical one. Build for trust first, capability second. A tool nobody uses is just expensive infrastructure.
